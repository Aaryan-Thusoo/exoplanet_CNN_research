\section{Detection Challenges} \label{sec:Detection_Challenges}

Many factors, including stellar variability and instrumental noise, can affect the accuracy of light curve analysis in transit photometry. These effects not only influence parameter estimation but can often lead to false positive detections. A false positive occurs when a signal is incorrectly interpreted to be a confirmation of a certain event. In the context of transit surveys, this happens when a model determines a transit-like signal to be evidence of a planet even though no planet is present in the system. Several physical and instrumental effects can produce signals that mimic transit patterns.

\subsection{Stellar Variability}

Stars exhibit time-variable activity across their surfaces. Sunspots reduce the local surface brightness while faculae lead to an increase in the surrounding region. The timescale and amplitude are dependent on factors such as effective temperature \citep{2012IAUS..285..364M}. When a transit passes over these active regions, this can cause small fluctuations in the transit profile \citep{2013A&A...556A..19O}. Active regions evolve and rotate across the stellar surface, introducing time-dependent variability. This rotational modulation can produce broad, quasi-periodic trends in the light curve that may obscure or imitate shallow transit signals. As a result, stellar variability can affect both the detection of transits and the estimation of planetary parameters derived from the light curve \citep{Bonomo_2009}.

\subsection{Instrumentation} 

Missions focused on transit observations must rely on precise differential photometry. The \textit{Kepler} mission \citep{2016RPPh...79c6901B}, for example, must detect signals as small as $\approx80$ parts per million (ppm) which corresponds to an Earth-sized planet transiting a Sun-like star \citep{2010ApJ...713L..92C}. Referring back to Equation~\ref{eq:flux_depth}, transit detections are favourable for systems with small stars with large planets. For a Sun-like star, a Neptune-sized planet produces a relative flux decrease on the order of $10^{-3}$. 

Achieving the necessary sensitivity to detect these signals is limited by several instrumental factors. Photon and detector noise as well as background flux contamination all affect the observations and must be accounted for \citep{2011ApJS..197....6G}. One important step in improving sensitivity is extended observation times, which works to increase signal-to-noise ratios. The need for greater precision led to space-based missions becoming the primary method for transit surveys over ground based photometry. 

In addition to fundamental photon and detector noise, there is astrophysical time series variability which introduces photometric deviations. As a result, the detection threshold cannot be described only using Gaussian white noise \citep{2006MNRAS.373..231P}. Often referred to as "red noise", this can arise from events such as thermal variations or stellar variability and may make it difficult to discern from genuine light curve trend. Transit depths and durations can be distorted by this noise, which can mimic shallow transit-like signals during analysis. As a result, the effective signal-to-red noise ratio relative to white-noise statistics is thus reduced, limiting confidence in exoplanet detections \citep{2006MNRAS.373..799C}. Appendix~\ref{sec:Signal_to_Red_Noise} describes the calculation for this signal-to-red-noise used in many transit detection studies. In practice, observed noise is composed of multiple contributions, including instrumental effects, intrinsic stellar variability in addition to Poisson noise. This forms a fundamental limitation of transit detection and thus careful de-trending, calibration and statistical analysis of photometric data is required. Beyond noise-related effects, transit detection is also limited by the temporal sampling of the observations.

The temporal resolution of observations is limited by the time series cadence. This limits how precisely we can observe the ingress, egress and total transit duration. Furthermore, interruptions in observations can introduce gaps that can disrupt the detection of patterns in transit signals \citep{10.1111/j.1365-2966.2010.17242.x}. The impact of these issues can vary in degree depending on the orbital period of the planet. These observation limits are particularly severe for exoplanets with long-periods as the overall number of observed transits is limited \citep{Zink_2018}. This increases the risk of missed detections or incorrect determination of orbital periods \citep{Christiansen_2016}. Consequently, cadence, duty cycle, and temporal coverage represent fundamental observational limitations that must be considered in transit survey design and data analysis. 

In wide-field transit surveys, nearby stars can introduce additional challenges due to photometric contamination. The photometric apertures used to encompass stellar flux can also gather light from nearby sources creating a blended signal. This crowding dilutes the measured transit depth, reducing the accuracy of potential exoplanet radii estimations \citep{Morton_2016}. Conversely, eclipsing binaries located within or near the photometric aperture can produce diluted eclipse signals with depths comparable to planetary transits. These scenarios produce astrophysical false positives that are difficult to distinguish using photometry alone. Mitigating these effects requires complementary observational techniques such as centroid motion measurements, high-resolution imaging, and statistical validation techniques to separate true planetary signals from blended stellar variability.

\subsection{Eclipsing Binaries}

A significant fraction of catalogued stars are found in binary star systems \citep{2010A&ARv..18...67T}. When the orbital plane is viewed near edge-on, the stars periodically appear to cross in front of one another, producing two distinct dips in the light curve. The deeper of the two eclipses is created by the brighter star being occulted and is referred to as the primary eclipse. The secondary eclipse occurs when the fainter star is obscured. Systems exhibiting this pattern are known as eclipsing binaries (EBs).

As both stellar objects emit light, EB systems produce two dips in the flux per orbital period. This results in flux drops being dependent on surface-brightness ratios as well as the projected area of overlap between their disks. Due to the geometry of the system, the stars can partially graze one another, creating a characteristic V-shape in the light curve \citep{2010exop.book...55W}. In the case of complete occultation, however, a flatter or U-shaped minimum is produced. Exoplanets with a high impact parameter (b) produce more grazing transits, which minimize the size of the flat/U-shaped portion of the light curve. In observational data, this produces a sharper dip that can resemble the V-shapes formed in eclipsing binaries. Another indicator of an EB is a slight increase in flux immediately before and after each eclipse. This occurs because the combined flux is greatest when the two stars appear next to one another in our point of view \citep{2011MNRAS.415.3921F}. Figure~\ref{fig:eclipsing_binary} shows a sample eclipsing binary using phase-folded light curves of KIC 4908495 and KIC 6466939 \citep{2025ApJ...988..259O}.

The mass ratio of eclipsing binary systems vary widely, leading to primary and secondary eclipses of different depths. However, in cases where the stars are similar in size, the eclipse depths may appear nearly identical. In such cases, this can be interpreted as a single repeating transit signal, leading to a false estimate of an exoplanet that is half of the true binary period \citep{fp_first_habitable_zone}. This ambiguity illustrates the broader role of eclipsing binaries as a major source of false positives in transit surveys.

Ground based surveys alongside the \textit{CoRoT} mission \cite{2006cosp...36.3749B} confirmed that both undiluted \citep{2008A&A...488L..47M, 2009IAUS..253..382A} and diluted \citep{2013A&A...553A..30T} eclipsing binaries dominate the population of false positives in transit surveys. \cite{2011ApJ...738..170M} investigated the probability that \textit{Kepler} transit signals could arise from such astrophysical false-positive scenarios. Using a Monte Carlo population synthesis model, they simulated the distribution of stellar systems in the \textit{Kepler} field along with possible configurations of eclipsing binaries and planetary systems that could reproduce transit-like signals. Focusing on candidates with planet radii between $0.5$ and $20R_{\oplus}$, their analysis estimated that approximately $90\%$ of close-in planet candidates had a false positive probability (FPP) below $10\%$, with a median FPP of $\approx5\%$. In contrast, \cite{2012A&A...545A..76S} used follow-up radial velocity observations with the SOPHIE spectrograph to measure a significantly larger FPP of $34.8\% \pm 6.5\%$ for close-in giant planet candidates with orbital periods $P\leq25$ days. This relatively high probability of misidentification showed the importance of properly accounting for eclipsing binary contamination when validating transit candidates.
