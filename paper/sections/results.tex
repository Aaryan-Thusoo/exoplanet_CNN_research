\section{Results}

This section presents the results of the binary and multiclass models for Kepler archive data. Starting with the binary convolutional neural network, the model attempted to learn the general differences between transits and eclipsing binary light curves. To examine and manage the range of eclipsing binaries, the multiclass CNN was used separating shallow and deep EBs. Lastly, the random forest models for both binary and multiclass cases were used to serve as a baseline for the quality of the CNNs.


\subsection{Binary CNN Training History}

 The model was trained over 14 epochs with no early stopping. During this time, the loss and accuracy of the training data were recorded at the end of each epoch. The validation data was not used in the updating of weights but was used to evaluate performance at the end of each epoch and recorded as well. This was done to evaluate improvements of the model over time to make sure to avoid under or over training. The results throughout the epoch are recorded to check learning progress and overfitting.

\subsection{Binary CNN Test Performance}

Experiments using higher or lower epochs led to a consistent trend of overfitting or underfitting. After the completion of the training, the model was evaluated on the test data to determine the model's quality. The test data was not provided to the model throughout training and thus the dataset was now useful for evaluating the models ability to detect class specific patterns. In total there were 1555 test instances used with there being a roughly equal split of transits and eclipsing binaries. After the evaluation of the model, the evaluation metrics were saved and are recorded below in Table~\ref{tab:metrics_table}. 

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c}
        Accuracy & 0.85 \\ \hline
        Precision & 0.80 \\ \hline
        Recall & 0.96\\ \hline
        F1 Score & 0.87\\ \hline
        ROC AUC & 0.89
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{The above table shows the final metrics of the model. The returned scores are good baselines to build stronger models off of.}
    \label{tab:metrics_table}
\end{table}

The accuracy shows the model correctly classified about $85\%$ of all the test light curves. Overall this is a reasonable performance indicating the model could be useful in further classifiers. On its own however, accuracy is not enough to confirm the model performs equally for both classes. The precision and recall metrics provide class specific context. A precision of $80\%$ means most of the instances the binary model classified as a transit were actual transiting exoplanets. However a non-negligable fraction of them were incorrectly labelled and $22\%$ of them were false transit detections. The recall of $96\%$ means the model recovered most of the true transits. While this is good these results may suggest a bias for the model to predict transiting exoplanets. 

The F1 score of $0.87$ sumarizes the balance of the precision and recall. This value indicates the model maintains a reasonable balance between recovering true transits and avoiding false transit detections, although precision remains the weaker component. Finally, the ROC AUC result of $0.89$ is strong and thus the model separates score ranks the classes reasonably well across classification thresholds. There is however still room for threshold tuning to potentially improve the model further.

It is shown in clearer view how the model misclassified a larger fraction of eclipsing binaries than transits and thus the network tended to predict instances as transits. 

\subsection{Binary CNN Analysis}

Of the 1555 test instances used, 231 were misclassified. There were 195 false positives and 36 false negatives and to understand why the model misclassified certain samples it is important to analyse the properties of these light curve instances. The first feature to analyse was the distribution of confidence for all test instances. Classifications with low confidence indicate the model prediction was close to the decision boundary. Misclassified instances with high confidence are of particular interest as the model assigns high confidence despite being incorrect. In a binary classification model, it is expected to see incorrect instances with low confidence near the 0.5 threshold. Thus it is important to analyse the incorrect cases which received higher confidence values. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{paper/figures/plots/bi_confidence_correct_vs_incorrect.png}
    \caption{A histogram showing the spread of frequency of each confidence value the binary model returned. All correct classifications shown in blue while incorrect classifications shown in orange. An interesting bump in mistakes by the model is found in the high 0.8 region.}
    \label{fig:conf_corr_vs_incorr_hist}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:conf_corr_vs_incorr_hist} shows the confidence distribution of all test instances split by correct and incorrect classifications. The majority of results are correct and mostly sit in the high confidence range which is to be expected based on the evaluation metrics. However there is a high number of high confidence misclassification which indicate the model is heavily struggling with certain instances. Thus it is important to analyse the properties of these light curves to find if any features of the light curve data is the main cause.

The features chosen to be looked into are labelled in Table~\ref{tab:kep_query_cols} and were collected during the query steps in the Methodology. Apart from the \texttt{kepid} label and \texttt{koi\_disposition}, the remaining columns all contain measured features of each light curve used. It is important to note that the calculated SNR developed in the methodology was added to this list for comparison against the \texttt{koi\_model\_snr}. Each of these features are plotted comparing their numbers to the confidence the model returned in its classification. By then looking trends it is possible to look for what features affect the model the most.

\subsubsection{Signal to Noise Ratio}

The first property evaluated was the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), because low SNR can make transit or eclipse signals harder to detect. Since the light curves used in this model did not undergo heavy filtering, binning, or additional cleaning beyond the preprocessing described in the methodology, SNR was examined as a possible contributor to model errors. Misclassified samples were separated into low-, medium-, and high-confidence ranges, and their calculated SNR values are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:calc_snr_boxplot}. The y-axis is log-scaled because the SNR values span several orders of magnitude. Figure~\ref{fig:calc_snr_boxplot_fp} shows the false positive cases, while Figure~\ref{fig:calc_snr_boxplot_fn} shows the false negative cases.

\subsubsection{Transit Depth} 

While the calculated and archive SNR measures show roughly similar results, transit depth is also a useful property to examine. Although the calculated SNR used this column, depth on its own provides information about the signal morphology and is less directly dependent on noise than SNR. Figure~\ref{fig:depth_boxplot} shows the boxplot results of the misclassified instances for depth. As the depth values were negative, the absolute value results are shown here. Therefore, larger absolute values correspond to deeper transit or eclipse depths. Figure~\ref{fig:depth_boxplot_fp} shows the most relevant trend in the high confidence group. As false positives are eclipsing binaries incorrectly classified as transits, this suggests that when the model is presented with eclipsing binaries with shallower dips, it has more difficulty separating them from transits.

\subsection{Multiclass CNN Test Performance}

Most metrics were calculated for the multiclass model as for the binary model. ROC AUC was not reported because the main comparison focused on accuracy, macro-averaged precision, macro-averaged recall, and macro-averaged F1 score. The accuracy was lower than the binary model, and the remaining metrics were also noticeably lower except for precision. As all the metrics are roughly in the $75\%$ range, this indicates moderate classification performance, but the CNN struggles to separate the classes in a substantial fraction of instances. This may reflect the similarities between shallow eclipsing binaries and transiting exoplanet signals.

\subsection{Results Summary} \textbf{(Make edits to this part still)}

Overall, the binary CNN achieved the strongest transit recovery, with high recall showing that most true transiting exoplanets were correctly identified. However, its lower precision and confusion matrix results show that a noticeable number of eclipsing binaries were incorrectly classified as transits. This suggests that the binary model tended to favour the transit class, especially in cases where eclipsing binary light curves had transit-like properties.

The multiclass CNN provided more detail on this limitation by separating eclipsing binaries into shallow and deep cases. Its results showed that the shallow eclipsing binary class was the most difficult to classify, with many shallow eclipsing binaries being predicted as transits. This supports the idea that the main source of confusion is not all eclipsing binaries equally, but specifically those with shallower eclipse depths that resemble exoplanet transits.

The confidence and depth analyses showed that low-confidence predictions were generally the most error-prone in both the binary and multiclass models. However, high-confidence mistakes were still present, indicating that model confidence alone is not enough to guarantee a correct classification. Transit depth helped explain some of the error patterns, but it did not fully account for all misclassifications.

The random forest models provided a useful baseline for comparison. Their performance showed that simple statistical and physical features, particularly depth-related features, contain meaningful information for separating the classes. Overall, the CNN models and random forest baselines together indicate that Kepler light curves contain enough structure to distinguish transits from eclipsing binaries, but the shallow eclipsing binary cases remain the main limitation for reliable classification.
