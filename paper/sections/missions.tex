\subsection{Kepler Mission}

The \textit{Kepler} mission was designed to identify exoplanets using high-precision transit photometry. NASA operated \textit{Kepler} between 2009 and 2013, during which it monitored more than 190,000 stars in a fixed 115 square degree field toward the Cygnus and Lyra constellations \citep{Kepler_Mission, 2016ApJS..224...12C}. Its continuous observations produced long-cadence light curves with a sampling interval of approximately 29.4 minutes, providing the time-series data used for transit detection and classification in this work \citep{Borucki_2010_Kepler}.

The \textit{Kepler} catalogue identifies observed stars using Kepler IDs (KepIDs), while stars showing transit-like signals are labelled as Kepler Objects of Interest (KOIs). KOIs that pass further vetting are classified as planet candidates. The final \textit{Kepler} catalogue demonstrated that detection performance depends strongly on orbital period and signal-to-noise ratio, with completeness and reliability decreasing for longer-period and lower-SNR planets \citep{2018ApJS..235...38T}. This is important for automated classification because shallow or low-SNR transit-like signals are more difficult to distinguish from astrophysical false positives.

Although \textit{Kepler} observed only a small region of the sky, it produced a large archive of high-precision light curves and transit-like events. This makes it a valuable dataset for developing and testing machine-learning methods that classify transit-like light curves efficiently and consistently.
